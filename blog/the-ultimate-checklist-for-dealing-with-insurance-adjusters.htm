The Ultimate Checklist for Dealing with Insurance Adjusters

  • Home
  • Blog
  • Blog Details

The interaction with an insurance adjuster is one of the most pivotal, yet daunting, moments following a disaster. Whether you're facing the aftermath of a wildfire, a catastrophic flood, a severe convective storm, or even a cyber-attack, the person on the other side of your claim holds significant power. In today’s world, where climate change is driving unprecedented natural disasters, supply chain issues are inflating rebuild costs, and geopolitical tensions impact everything from lumber prices to automotive parts, navigating an insurance claim requires more than just filling out forms. It demands strategy, documentation, and a clear understanding of the modern pressures on the insurance industry itself. This ultimate checklist is your tactical guide to leveling the playing field.

Understanding the Modern Adjuster's Landscape

Before you even pick up the phone, it's crucial to understand the context in which today's insurance adjusters operate.

The Pressure of "The New Normal" in Catastrophes

Insurance companies are reeling from what they now call "secondary perils" – frequent, severe localized events like hailstorms, wildfires, and inland flooding. The historic losses from events like Hurricane Ian or the Lahaina wildfires have led to tightened policies, higher deductibles, and increased scrutiny on every claim. Adjusters are under immense pressure to accurately assess damage while controlling costs for their employer. They are often handling hundreds of claims simultaneously, especially after a large-scale disaster. This doesn't make them adversaries, but it does mean they are efficiency-driven and may initially lean towards a standard, rather than a bespoke, assessment of your unique situation.

The Rise of Technology and Remote Adjusting

Gone are the days when an adjuster always spent hours at your property. Today, you might be asked to submit a claim via an app, upload dozens of photos and videos, or participate in a video call walkthrough. While this can speed up the process, it can also miss subtle, hidden damage like moisture intrusion behind walls or foundation shifts. Understanding this shift is key: your evidence must be so comprehensive that it compensates for the lack of prolonged physical presence.

The Pre-Contact Preparation Checklist

Your success is determined before the conversation even begins.

Document Everything, Meticulously and Digitally

  • Pre-Loss Evidence: If you haven't already, use your phone right now to walk through your home and business, recording a high-resolution video tour. Open drawers, film model and serial numbers of appliances, show the condition of roofing and flooring. Store this in the cloud (e.g., Google Drive, Dropbox).
  • Post-Loss Evidence: After the incident, safety permitting, photograph and video every single angle of damage. Get wide shots for context and tight close-ups for detail. Do not move items or begin repairs unless necessary to prevent further damage (e.g., putting a tarp on a roof).
  • Create a Master Inventory List: For any lost or damaged items, list the item, description, age, approximate purchase price, and replacement cost. Attach any receipts, credit card statements, or even photos that show you owned the item.

Know Your Policy Inside and Out

Locate your full policy document, not just the declarations page. Key sections to review: * Coverage Limits and Sublimits: What is the total for your dwelling? What are the limits for personal property, additional living expenses (ALE), or code upgrades? * Deductibles: Is it a flat rate or a percentage? Has it changed recently? * Exclusions and Endorsements: What is specifically excluded? (e.g., flood, earthquake, sewer backup). Do you have any endorsements for valuables or business equipment? * Duties After a Loss: Your policy is a contract. It will list your obligations, such as reporting the loss promptly and mitigating further damage. Follow these to the letter.

The Interaction Checklist: During the Adjuster's Visit or Call

This is the main event. Conduct yourself professionally and proactively.

Communication Protocol

  • Be Prompt and Professional: Return calls and emails promptly. Keep a log of every interaction: date, time, adjuster's name, and summary of discussion.
  • Stick to the Facts: Do not speculate, guess, or offer opinions on cause. "The tree fell through the roof during the windstorm" is factual. "I think the roof was probably weak anyway" is harmful speculation.
  • Do Not Admit Fault or Downplay Damage: Avoid phrases like "It could have been worse" or "It's just a little water damage." State the facts as they are.
  • You Are Not Required to Give a Recorded Statement Immediately: It is within your rights to say, "I would prefer to have my documentation fully prepared before providing a recorded statement to ensure accuracy."

The Site Visit Strategy

  • Do Not Be Alone: Have a trusted witness present, such as a contractor, public adjuster, or knowledgeable friend/family member. They can take notes and provide moral support.
  • Walk with the Adjuster: Point out all damage, but let them see it. Do not let them conduct a solo, abbreviated inspection.
  • Discuss Scope, Not Cost: Your role is to ensure every bit of damage is noted. The adjuster's role is to assign a cost. Say, "The water extended across the entire hallway and into these two bedrooms," not "This will cost $20,000 to fix."
  • Ask Questions: "What is your assessment of the foundation?" "Will you be including code upgrade coverage?" "How are you accounting for current material cost inflation in your estimate?"

The Post-Visit and Negotiation Checklist

The first estimate is rarely the final word.

Decoding the Estimate and Requesting Review

  • Get a Detailed, Line-Item Estimate: Do not accept a lump-sum check or a vague summary. You are entitled to a detailed breakdown (e.g., Xactimate or similar software report).
  • Scrutinize the Line Items: Compare the adjuster's estimate with contractor bids. Are the quantities sufficient? (e.g., enough square footage of drywall, lineal feet of fencing). Are the unit prices reflective of current local labor and material costs? Are all damaged items listed?
  • Submit a Formal Rebuttal: If items are missing or underpriced, respond in writing. Attach your contractor's detailed bid, photos of specific missed damage, and documentation of current material costs (e.g., printouts from local suppliers). Be specific, polite, and data-driven.

When to Bring in Reinforcements

  • Hire a Reputable Contractor: A good contractor can provide a binding bid that serves as a powerful negotiation tool.
  • Consider a Public Adjuster: For large or complex claims, a licensed public adjuster works for you, not the insurance company. They typically charge a percentage of the final settlement but often secure significantly higher payouts that outweigh their fee.
  • Consult an Attorney: If you suspect bad faith tactics (unreasonable delays, denial without investigation, failure to communicate), consult an attorney specializing in insurance law.

Special Considerations for Today's World

  • Climate-Driven Claims: For wildfire or flood damage, be prepared for arguments about "maintenance" vs. "covered peril." Prove the direct cause was the covered event. For mold resulting from floodwater, understand your policy's mold sublimit.
  • Inflation and Supply Chain (IFG): Use the term "IFG" (Inflation and Supply Chain) in your negotiations. Argue that the adjuster's estimate must use current, not historical, pricing. Provide evidence.
  • Cyber and Identity Theft Claims: For these policies, documentation is everything. Police reports, IT forensic reports, and logs of fraudulent transactions are essential.
  • Additional Living Expenses (ALE): Keep every receipt for hotels, meals, laundry, and pet boarding. In a major disaster, rental markets tighten, and ALE costs soar. Your policy should cover the increased cost of comparable living arrangements.

The relationship with your insurance adjuster is a negotiation, not a surrender. By entering the process prepared, documented, and informed about the macro-forces at play, you shift from being a supplicant to being a strategic partner in restoring your property and your life. Your claim is not just about repairing walls; it's about securing the financial resources to rebuild in an increasingly uncertain world. This checklist is your blueprint for ensuring that rebuild is complete, fair, and on your terms.
